Points That Reach a Lineup

A player's trade value is his points above the waiver line, times how often those points actually land in a starting lineup. This page walks that second part, one step at a time, on real players. Change the player or the inputs and every picture recomputes.

Defaults are the measured values: missed-game rate from games healthy starters actually missed in 2015–2025 (nflverse), uncertainty from the spread across ESPN, CBS rest of season and Razzball plus week-to-week drift, bye share from the 2026 schedule. Moving the week shrinks the bye share and the drift.

Step 1. Where the lines are

Twelve teams fill their dedicated slots, then the flex goes to whoever is best left over. The first player not drafted as a starter sets the starter line; the first player nobody rosters sets the waiver line. A player's value above waivers is his projection minus the waiver line. Nothing below the waiver line is worth anything, because anyone can have it for free.

Step 2. Does he play at all?

Two things keep a player out of every lineup regardless of talent: his bye, and injury or benching. Byes are known from the schedule. The injury rate is measured from the games healthy starters actually missed over eleven seasons, 2015 to 2025. Together they give his availability.

Step 3. When he plays, will he be projected to start?

Lineups are set on projections, before the games are played. So the question is not whether he will outscore the starters on Sunday, but whether his projection will sit above the starter line when the manager sets the lineup. Today's projection is the best guess, but projections move: the three sources disagree with each other now, and each one drifts week to week as roles and health change. That spread is the bell curve, centred on his current projection. The part above the starter line is his chance of being projected to start. The part between the two lines is where he is projected as a bench player of some depth. The part below the waiver line counts for nothing. What happens on Sunday is a separate, much larger swing (Step 8) and is deliberately not in this curve.

Step 4. If he is on the bench, how often does he get in anyway?

A bench player plays when enough starters ahead of him on his fantasy team are out. Each starter is out with the probability from Step 2 (bye or injury). The first player on the bench needs one starter out; the second needs two out at once. With more starters per team at a position, there are more chances for one of them to be missing.

Step 5. The expected lineup share

Put the steps together. Of every point above the waiver line, the share that reaches a lineup is availability × (chance he is projected to start + chance he is projected at depth 1 × fill-in rate for depth 1 + chance he is projected at depth 2 × fill-in rate for depth 2). The rest is lost: weeks he sits on a bench behind healthy starters, or is out himself.

Step 6. From shares to a position's curve, and where the bench share comes from

Every player's surplus is split into a start-worthy part and a fill-in part. Add up all the fill-in parts at the position and you have the share of value that exists only because starters miss games. That sum is the bench share. It is not a slider any more; it is what the data says. The chart compares the position's curve today (every surplus point counts equally, then a fixed 15% is carved off for the bench) with the curve after (each player's surplus times his own share), both at the same total.

Step 7. How the bench share moves through the season

Two inputs change with the calendar today. The bye share falls as byes pass (it is zero from Week 14). The uncertainty shrinks as fewer weeks are left for a player's projection to drift. Both push bench value down, gently: the bench tier goes from 8.7% at Week 5 to 7.7% at Week 17. Why a shorter window doesn't lower this week's share

Each week a healthy starter at this position is out with the same chance (the missed-game slider). That per-week chance is what the share uses, so it doesn't change as the season runs down. What does shrink is the chance he misses at least one of the remaining games, which is what a manager holding a backup is really insuring against. The bars show both for the position and rate selected above.

Trade value here is a slice of a fixed pie each week, so the right-hand bars never enter it. A horizon term would let them in, and that is the main way bench value would fall toward zero by the playoffs (JEG-528).

Proposed: league weeks on the dashboard

Three light inputs: the content week, your league's last regular-season week (default 14) and playoff weeks (default 15 to 17), plus what to optimize for: the whole season, the regular season, or the playoffs only. Each one changes the bye share and the drift window, and the playoff setting also uses the injury rate measured in playoff weeks. Ticket: JEG-527. Measured at Week 5 the bench tier barely moves: 8.65% today, 8.65% whole season, 8.71% regular season, 8.71% playoffs. So the inputs matter for what the numbers mean and for the dashboard, not for the decision.

Step 8. Week-to-week noise, which this page does not price

Everything above is about where a player's projection sits, which is what a manager decides on. On any given Sunday a starter's actual score swings far more than projections move. Measured over 2015–2025, the weekly points of a starter vary by this much around his own average. Pass catchers are noisier than quarterbacks, as suspected. This is why a low-end starter is sometimes projected below a high-end bench player in a given week. Pricing that swap needs stored weekly projections, which is a separate piece of work.

Step 9. What is recommended, and the alternatives

For each alternative: why it is not recommended, whether what it was after is handled somewhere else, and whether it comes back later.

Recommended

Share of surplus, by probability

Steps 1 to 6 as shown. Bench tier holds 8.6% of the pie at 12 teams. A starter-level point is worth 1.6× a bench-level point at running back, 2.9× at wide receiver. No calibration solve, so no silent clamps.

Measured, not chosen

Pay for the size of the upside too

Why not used. It also pays for how big a bench player would be if he became a starter. That gives back most of the starter premium: the bench tier rises to 12.0% and the premium falls to 1.2×. Players near the starter line get valued above their whole surplus. And it is driven by the uncertainty number, the least measured input.

Handled elsewhere? Yes. The upside becomes the Stash signal: each bench player's chance of being projected to start, shown beside his value, not added to it. One gap: a handcuff's jump when his NFL starter goes down is in neither (Step 10).

Future? Yes, as a separate option-value series on the same scale but outside DDF Value, once uncertainty is measured from real projection errors (math agenda MR-21, JEG-530). The handcuff jump goes into the Stash signal first (MR-26, JEG-526).

Measured, not chosen

Keep the slices, set the budget

Why not used. It gets the same 8.6% bench tier only by forcing it. Every player keeps the same kink at the starter line, and every starter's points below the line are still paid at the bench rate. There is no per-player lineup share to show, and it needs a second rule to carry the shares into the weights.

Handled elsewhere? Nothing is lost. Its bench share is the chosen rule's by construction, and the chosen rule also gives each player his own share.

Future? No. It is a fallback only if the chosen rule cannot land in the pipeline rewrite in time, because it is the smallest code change.

What ships first

Keep the fixed 15%

Where it stands. You set the bench share at 15% with slices on the pipeline rewrite (JEG-508), and that is what ships first. The earlier warning about 39% to 49% no longer applies: that was the "source-implied" version, which you rejected.

Why it isn't the recommendation. The measured bench tier is 8.0% to 9.9%, not 15%. At 15%, a starter-level point is often worth less than a bench-level point (0.7× to 1.9×). And one number is wrong for most settings: about 9.6% at 8 teams, 8.0% to 8.6% at 12 and 14, falling through the season.

Future? The question is whether the 15% becomes a computed readout from your league settings, with the slider kept as an override. Decision: JEG-533.

Inputs barely matter to the answer: halving or raising the missed-game rate and the uncertainty by 50% keeps the bench tier between 8.2% and 9.1%. Try it with the sliders above. Spec: docs/methodology.md, "Expected-starts value". Decision ticket: JEG-533.

The handcuff, in numbers

The share asks "how often is a starter ahead of him on an average fantasy team out?" A handcuff is held for something else: the jump he makes when his own NFL team's lead back is out. Over 2015–2025, backups scored 7.2 points per game with the lead back playing and 12.8 with him out. Behind a top-12 lead back the jump was 6.9 to 13.4, and above the 12-team starter line about 55% of the time.

The bell curve in Step 3 sits on the backup's own projection, so it cannot produce a jump that size. A pure handcuff below the waiver line is worth zero in the pie and zero in the Stash signal. At Week 5 that includes Sione Vaki behind Gibbs (4.16 points per game) and Justice Hill behind Henry (4.74). What is missing for a top-12 back's handcuff is about 0.8 points of surplus per week reaching a lineup. That is the same as Keaton Mitchell's entire value at RB44, so it is not small. The proposal (MR-26) is to add it to the Stash signal from the depth chart and this measured jump, outside the pie, as with other upside. Ticket: JEG-526.
