Who checks the trade charts?

Every fantasy trade argument ends the same way: somebody posts a chart. But a trade chart is a model with opinions baked in. How much is a running back worth next to a receiver? What changes in superflex? Do two quarters make a dollar? We put four widely available redraft trade charts and one open dynasty chart through the same tests on the same week, measured against one league-aware reference: DDF Value.

How we graded

We used the numbers each chart published for week 9, as our pipeline saved them, and asked seven questions of each. Most tests use only the chart's own numbers: does a number change when the scoring changes, how many players does the chart list, how often do two lesser players add up to a star. Where a test needs a reference, it uses DDF Value: the four redraft charts' values, each adjusted for league settings and replacement level, then averaged. DDF Value is the reference line, not a contestant, so it has no grades. Because it is built from the same charts, these tests measure how each chart differs from that league-aware consensus, not from an independent truth; where a finding rests on first principles instead, we say so.

The scale is simple: Strong, OK, Weak, or Not measured when we lack the data or the test does not apply. The exact rule for each grade sits under the table. A grade describes one snapshot, not a chart's whole season.

1. Position balance: the gap opens in Standard

Of all the value in a league, how much goes to each position? We measured each chart's share of value above waivers by position and compared it with DDF Value's position budget. In a Half PPR, one-QB league every chart is within about 6 points of it (FantasyPros 4, FantasyCalc 5, CBS Sports 5.2, USA Today 5.9). CBS Sports, FantasyCalc and USA Today lean toward receivers (5.2, 3.9 and 3.6 points); FantasyPros leans toward running backs (4).

Press the scoring buttons for the bigger pattern. In Standard, DDF Value gives running backs 55.7% of the value and the Analyst charts (the average of CBS Sports, USA Today and FantasyPros) 51.1%; in Full PPR the two are 41.3% and 41.1%. The charts' mix changes less with scoring than the reference does. FantasyPros, with one column for every format, is the closest of the four in Standard (2.7 points). DynastyProcess gives receivers 46.7% in Half PPR, as a chart that prices future seasons can.

This test measures each chart's numbers inside one league structure: how many backs and receivers start in each format is the reference's allocation, so a chart's share moves with scoring even when its numbers do not (FantasyPros' running backs go from 58.4% in Standard to 45.9% in Full PPR on identical numbers). It compares charts with each other and with DDF Value in that same structure.

On running backs. Backs miss more games, and DDF Value's league adjustment already counts on it: it expects running backs to miss 15.3% of games and receivers 10.5%. A chart that wants a bigger safety margin than that is making a judgment, not an error.

2. The shape of the curve: how fast does value fall?

Line up every player from best to worst and divide by the #1 player. A steep curve says stars are rare and expensive. A flat one says depth is nearly as good. By the 24th player, CBS Sports has fallen to 38% of its #1, DDF Value to 40%, FantasyCalc to 46%, FantasyPros to 51%, DynastyProcess to 55% and USA Today only to 65%. Toggle the scoring and watch which lines bend. Two of them never do.

3. Does the chart know your scoring?

Switch from Standard to Full PPR and the order of players should change: a running back who catches 60 passes should pass one who does not. That follows from the scoring rules alone. We took the top 36 running backs, receivers and tight ends on each list and counted how many change places. USA Today moves 59% and CBS Sports 32%, both from separate published columns per format; DDF Value, the reference, moves 37%. FantasyCalc changes every number but none of its top 99 change places within their position: running backs and receivers each get one multiplier, and tight ends and quarterbacks vary a little more without reordering. FantasyPros changes nothing, and neither does DynastyProcess, which publishes one value set and says it assumes 12-team PPR.

How we checked that this is them, not us. FantasyCalc: our pipeline makes one request per format to its public values endpoint (ppr=0 for Standard, ppr=1 for Full PPR, 12 teams, one QB) and stores the published number untouched; the multiplier is in those raw responses, not in any adjustment of ours. We repeated the two requests live on 2026-11-07 and got the same pattern. In 8 captures from 2026-09-29 to 2026-11-07, only 1, the oldest (2026-09-29), reordered players (10 of the top 36 running backs), so this looks like a change on FantasyCalc's side around the start of October. An independent check, run separately from ours on 2026-11-07 with the same public parameters (ppr=0, 0.5 and 1; numQbs=1; numTeams=12) and a fresh read of the FantasyPros article, reproduced both findings. FantasyPros: the week 9 article's four tables carry one Value column (plus 2QB and tight end premium columns) and no scoring selector (read 2026-11-07), and its numbers were identical across formats in every weekly snapshot from week 5 to week 9.

A fair note for CBS Sports and USA Today: their quarterback numbers are the same in every format because neither publishes a quarterback column per scoring format. We use CBS Sports' one-QB 4-point column and USA Today's one-QB column; neither chart's 6-point passing touchdown column is used here.

4. Superflex: the quarterback discount

All four redraft charts publish superflex or 2QB numbers for 32 to 38 quarterbacks, enough for a 12-team league. Credit where due. Move to superflex and those four lift quarterbacks from about 8% of all value to between 22.1% and 34.6%. DDF Value, which builds its superflex prices from these same charts' superflex lists and then adjusts for the extra lineup slot and replacement level, gives them 34.9%. FantasyPros sits next to it; CBS Sports is 12.8 points below. A superflex league puts about 24 quarterbacks in lineups each week out of 32 NFL starters, so the direction of the move follows from the lineup rules; its size is where the charts differ. This test measures quarterback share against the league-aware reference; it does not measure what managers pay in real superflex trades.

5. Depth: does the chart reach the end of your bench?

Every position has a waiver line: the value of the best player you could pick up for free once every team has filled its starters and bench. League size and roster slots set it. With our default lineup, a 12-team league rosters about 20 quarterbacks, 65 running backs, 62 receivers and 21 tight ends, so the line sits at the next player at each position (10 teams: 18, 59, 48, 15; 14 teams: 24, 74, 73, 25). Value above that line is value above waivers, the basis of DDF Value.

A chart's list should end near that line. If it stops short, real roster value is left unpriced. If it reaches past, it puts a price on players who, in this league, the waiver wire could replace. The depth grade counts only stopping short; reaching past is reported here, because a deeper league or a longer horizon can justify it. Here is where each list ends in a 12-team league.

  • CBS Sports stops short at every position: 13 QBs, 43 RBs, 43 WRs and 11 TEs. Its last running back, Devin Singletary, is still worth 8.8% of its top back, and 22 rostered backs get no number at all. CBS Sports covers them with a rule (any unlisted player is worth no more than four points), so in a bench-for-bench trade it cannot tell your 50th back from your 60th.
  • USA Today reaches past the line at every position. 5.3% of all its value goes to players beyond it, the most of any chart, and its waiver-level tight end is still worth 11.1% of its top tight end. In our 12-team league, that is value placed on players the waiver wire could replace; in a deeper league some of it would be in play.
  • FantasyPros is mixed: past the line at QB and WR, short at RB (52 of 65), near it at TE. Its receiver at the line is still worth 14.5% of its top receiver, so in our 12-team league it places real value on receivers at the waiver line.
  • FantasyCalc puts the least value past the line of the charts that reach past it: near the line at RB, and past it at QB, WR and TE only with players worth almost nothing on its scale; just 0.6% of its value sits past the line.
  • DynastyProcess lists nearly everyone, as a dynasty list for a deeper league would: its method page assumes two FLEX spots and about 300 rostered players, against the 168 in our redraft league. 3.1% of its value sits past our line, which describes coverage against our league, not a pricing error.

6. The 2-for-1 test

Here is where curve shape stops being academic. Add up two chart numbers and compare them with one, and you have priced a 2-for-1. But the team that takes two players needs a roster spot, and a freely available player at each position has some value on most charts. Subtract each player's replacement value (the waiver-level value at his position) and the math changes; this does not model the specific player your team would cut. Across every comparison of a pair ranked 25 to 60 with a top-12 player (630 pairs times 12 stars, all weighted equally), on FantasyPros 68% favor the pair on raw numbers and 45% after the subtraction. On USA Today, 61% and 42%. CBS Sports and FantasyCalc are steep enough that the subtraction barely matters. CBS Sports also states a rule for lopsided deals: take 10% off when one side gets at least two more players [1]. DynastyProcess sits in between: 27% raw, 17% after.

Try one yourself. The default is a sample week 9 comparison on raw chart numbers; tick the box to subtract each player's replacement value. The box works on each chart's numbers as a share of its own #1 player, which keeps every chart's ratios.

7. Where the charts disagree

The good news: the charts mostly agree on order. Across the 109 players all four list, rank correlations run from 0.831 to 0.925. The fights are about price, and about a handful of players. Pick a chart to see where it parts from the other three. Points above the dashed line are players this chart ranks higher than the other three do; points below, lower.

8. Weekly change

Our saved weekly snapshots differ for every source from week 5 to week 9, and every one of them moved its top players. The analyst charts keep about a quarter to a half of their numbers exactly the same from one week to the next. FantasyCalc changed every one of those numbers every week. This test measures week-to-week change in what we saved; it does not measure how fast a source reacts to news.

9. Age: what a dynasty value pays for

Our snapshot has ages for one source only: DynastyProcess publishes them in its open data file. So we can ask a fair question of it. Among the 149 of our top 150 players that DynastyProcess lists with an age, the 44 under 25 rank 3.8 places higher at their position on DynastyProcess than on DDF Value, on average. Players 30 and over come out at -3.6 places. That is the pattern you would expect from values that price future seasons, and it makes DynastyProcess a poor fit for a redraft trade: on the 109 players all four redraft charts list, its rank correlation with them runs from 0.524 (FantasyPros) to 0.727 (FantasyCalc), against 0.831 to 0.925 among the redraft charts themselves.

What each headline should be

For each finding: what we would expect, the gap, why, and how to check it. Two expectations follow from first principles alone; the others use DDF Value as the reference, which measures distance from the league-aware consensus of the same charts.

1. FantasyPros and scoring formats

Should be: players change places between Standard and Full PPR; the two charts with a column per format move 32% to 59% of their top 36, DDF Value 37%. Shows: 0%. Gap: no movement at all.

Why: a catch is worth a point in Full PPR and nothing in Standard, and players catch very different numbers of passes, so the order has to move. This is a first principle, independent of DDF Value. Check it on the CBS Sports chart, whose NON and PPR columns order players differently. For your trade: in Standard or Full PPR, use a chart with a column for your format.

2. FantasyCalc and scoring formats

Should be: the same movement within each position. Shows: 0 of 99 reorder; every top back gets about 0.994 (spread 0.001). Gap: the order within a position never moves.

Why: one factor per position can shift backs against receivers, but it cannot lift a pass-catching back over a runner; only player-level scoring can. That is arithmetic, independent of DDF Value. Check it with the two FantasyCalc links in the headline. For your trade: FantasyCalc's format switch changes how positions trade against each other, not how two backs compare.

3. The 2-for-1 test

Should be: few pairs of mid-tier players worth more than a top-12 star once each player's replacement value is subtracted; DDF Value, the reference, has 9%. Shows: after the subtraction, FantasyPros 45%, USA Today 42%, FantasyCalc 8%, CBS Sports 2%. Gap: 36 and 33 points above the reference for FantasyPros and USA Today.

Why: a lineup starts only so many players, so two players are worth what each adds above a free pickup, and on a flat chart that surplus is spread thin. The first-principles part, subtracting replacement value, does not depend on DDF Value; the size of the gap does. Check it on the FantasyPros chart with any two players ranked around 30th. For your trade: on a flat chart, a 2-for-1 that looks fair usually favors the side getting the star.

4. Quarterbacks in superflex

Should be: about 34.9% of all value, DDF Value's share. Shows: CBS Sports 22.1%, USA Today 26%, FantasyCalc 26.5%, FantasyPros 34.6%. Gap: 12.8, 8.9, 8.4 and 0.3 points below.

Why: with about 24 quarterbacks starting each week, the replacement quarterback is a backup, so starters gain a lot; that direction is a first principle. The size comes from DDF Value's league adjustment of the charts' own superflex lists, a modeling judgment that real superflex trade prices would test. For your trade: on CBS Sports, a superflex quarterback is priced well below the league-aware consensus, which helps a buyer.

5. The top tight end

Should be: about 42% of the #1 player, DDF Value's figure. Shows: USA Today 80%, FantasyCalc 63%, CBS Sports 42%, FantasyPros 37%. Gap: USA Today 38 and FantasyCalc 21 points above; CBS Sports level; FantasyPros -5.

Why: a league starts one tight end per team and the position falls off quickly after the top few, so the best tight end is worth a lot but well short of a top running back; that shape is a first principle. The exact figure is DDF Value's, the consensus of these charts after league adjustment. Check it on the USA Today charts and the CBS Sports chart. For your trade: on USA Today an elite tight end prices close to a top player; the other charts and the reference put him well below.

Chart by chart

What each chart does well, where it falls short, and what it says about its own method. Method facts come only from pages the source published, read on 2026-11-07. Where we could not find or load a method page, we say so and make no claim.

CBS Sports Analyst chart

  • Does well: separate Standard, Half and Full PPR columns that really reorder players; a 2QB column; the steepest curve through the top 24, so 2-for-1 sums hold up; written rules for lopsided deals and unlisted players.
  • Falls short: lists 110 players, which stops well short of a 12-team bench; leans furthest toward receivers against DDF Value (5.2 points in Half PPR); the lowest quarterback share in superflex (22.1%).
  • Method, in its own words: Dave Richard's chart is based on expected future performance, upcoming schedule and public sentiment, and doubles as rest-of-season rankings. League size: not documented. [1]

USA Today Analyst chart

  • Does well: the deepest of the analyst charts (271 players), so it prices bench players itself; separate scoring columns that reorder 59% of the top 36; a superflex column for 38 quarterbacks.
  • Falls short: the flattest curve (the 24th player is worth 65% of the #1), so sums of two players flatter the side getting more; its top tight end at 80% of its #1 is far above every other chart.
  • Method: not documented in any page we could load. Its site did not allow our reader on 2026-11-07, so we describe only its published table as our pipeline saved it. [2]

FantasyPros Analyst chart

  • Does well: a position mix within 4 points of DDF Value in Half PPR and the closest of the four in Standard (2.7); 2QB values for quarterbacks and a tight end premium column; prints each player's bye week.
  • Falls short: one Value column for every scoring format, so it cannot tell Standard from Full PPR; a flat curve: in 68% of pair-versus-star comparisons the mid-tier pair comes out ahead on raw numbers.
  • Method: the article says its analysts combined their rankings to make the values, and that "Values will be different league-to-league." Scoring format and league size: not stated. [3]

FantasyCalc Live market

  • Does well: takes league size as a setting; a 2QB setting with values for every player; a steep curve, so 2-for-1 sums barely need the roster-spot correction; new numbers for its top players every week.
  • Falls short: in our captures since early October its scoring switch rescales each position instead of reordering within it (section 3 shows how we checked): 0 of the top 99 move; leans toward receivers (3.9 points above DDF Value in Half PPR); lists 60 running backs where a 12-team league rosters about 65.
  • Method: its method page did not load for us on 2026-11-07, so we make no claim about how its values are built. Its public values request takes dynasty or redraft, number of quarterbacks, number of teams and PPR. [4]

DynastyProcess Dynasty, open data

  • Does well: the only source here that publishes both its formula and its data under an open license (GPL-3.0), so every number can be checked; 2QB values for 90 quarterbacks; the longest list of the graded sources (604 players we could match, against 110 to 271 for the charts); new values every week.
  • Falls short: one value set for every scoring format; it prices future seasons, so it is a poor fit for a redraft trade (it gives receivers 46.7% of the value in Half PPR, and its rank correlation with the redraft charts runs from 0.524 to 0.727 on the players all four list).
  • Method, in its own words: Value = 10,500 × e−0.0235 × rank, where rank is the FantasyPros dynasty expert consensus; 12-team PPR; it states no age adjustment of its own; method page last updated 2020-06-25. Its formula puts the 24th player at 58% of the #1. [8]

The big names we could not test

For the charts we grade, this article shows each chart's numbers only as shares of its own #1 player, as ranks and as summary measures, not as published. We did not collect data from these four. KeepTradeCut's terms prohibit automated data collection and redistributing its content [11]; Footballguys' terms restrict copying and reuse without express written permission [12]; Draft Sharks publishes no data license or reuse terms we could find, and its league-specific values come from its Draft War Room [10]. So these stay method-only, with no grades. We also looked at Dynasty Nerds but found no published method or data terms we could read, so it is left out [13].

KeepTradeCut Crowd votes

  • Users answer a daily keep, trade or cut question; the answers are combined with an ELO algorithm. A second mode builds values from real dynasty trades with a proprietary algorithm, and a third blends the two. [7]
  • Crowd values update every 10 minutes. Its own stated limits: trade data lags breaking news, and crowd values can overreact.
  • League settings the values assume: not documented on the page we loaded.

Footballguys Analyst chart

  • Dan Hindery's dynasty chart starts from points per game above replacement, discounted over a career, and is checked against about 90,000 Sleeper dynasty trades. [9]
  • Its base setting is superflex, Full PPR; the author says it is maintained by hand and that he adjusts it at times.

Draft Sharks Model

  • Its dynasty values come from models trained on player data since 1999, with aging curves by position, expressed as value above replacement. [10]
  • Baseline: 12 teams, with 1QB, superflex and tight end premium versions.

What this means for your trade

  1. In a 2-for-1, subtract each player's replacement value. On FantasyPros and USA Today, adding two numbers makes depth look rich. CBS Sports and FantasyCalc are steep enough that it matters less.
  2. Check the scoring before you quote a chart. FantasyPros has one number for every format, and none of FantasyCalc's top 99 running backs, receivers and tight ends change places within their position between Standard and Full PPR. CBS Sports and USA Today publish a column per format; use the right one.
  3. In superflex, check the quarterback price. The charts give quarterbacks 22 to 35% of all value; CBS Sports sits furthest below the league-aware reference. If you are buying a quarterback, a low chart price helps your pitch.
  4. Expect agreement on order, not on price. The charts rank players much the same; they disagree about exchange rates, especially for tight ends and for the second half of your roster.

How we tested

  • One snapshot: week 9 of 2026. Analyst charts published 2026-11-03 (saved by 2026-11-04); FantasyCalc pulled 2026-11-07 (the weekly-change table uses its 2026-11-03 snapshot); DynastyProcess values of 2026-11-06. Another week can grade differently.
  • League: 12 teams, our default lineup (QB, 2 RB, 2 WR, TE, FLEX, 7 bench), Half PPR unless noted. Depth is also shown at 10 and 14 teams.
  • Reference: DDF Value, the four redraft charts adjusted for league settings and replacement level and averaged. It measures each chart against the league-aware consensus of the same charts, not against an independent truth. Its position budget averages the position mixes of every source in our pipeline, including the projection sets our model uses as inputs; those projections are not trade charts and are not graded here.
  • Short charts: where a chart lists fewer players than a league rosters, DDF Value fills in the missing players from the other charts (67 for CBS Sports this week). CBS Sports says an unlisted player is worth up to four points (7.1% of its #1); the fill-in puts its waiver-level running back at 0.6% of its #1.
  • League sizes: every chart is saved at 12 teams; 8, 10 and 14 teams are derived, including for FantasyCalc, which offers other team counts.
  • Data checks: USA Today's week 9 capture carried an amber data-check flag; it is still part of DDF Value.
  • Not tested: rookies and injuries (our injury list is newer than the charts). Ages exist only in DynastyProcess's data, so the age test covers one chart. DynastyProcess is matched by name with our standard rule: 604 matched, 9 not, none worth even 0.1% of its #1.
  • Grades: the thresholds are ours and written under the scorecard. Methods: statements come from each source's own pages, read 2026-11-07; where a source does not document something, we say so.
