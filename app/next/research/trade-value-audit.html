DATA DRIVEN FOOTBALLDraft

Research · Week 9, 2026

Who checks the trade charts?

Every fantasy trade argument ends the same way: somebody posts a chart. But a trade chart is a model with opinions baked in. How much is a running back worth next to a receiver? What changes in superflex? Do two quarters make a dollar? We put the four charts most people use, three projection sets and a dynasty tool whose values are open data, through the same tests on the same week. Then we put ourselves through them too.

How we graded, and why you should not take our word for it

We used the numbers each source published for week 9, as our pipeline saved them, and asked seven questions of each. Most tests lean on the source's own numbers: does a number change when the scoring changes, how many players does the chart list, how often do two lesser players add up to a star. Two tests use our model as the yardstick, and two more (depth and the roster-spot subtraction) use its roster counts. The yardstick is projections from ESPN, CBS rest-of-season and Razzball, turned into value above waivers by the Data Driven Football model. That model is a point of view, not the truth. Where it decides the answer, we say so.

The scale is simple: Strong, OK, Weak, or Not measured when we lack the data or the test does not apply. The exact rule for each grade sits under the table. A grade describes one snapshot, not a source's whole season.

1. Position balance: charts dress for Full PPR

Start with the big question: of all the value in a league, how much goes to each position? We measured each chart's share of value above waivers by position, the same way our model weighs any source. In a Half PPR, one-QB league, CBS Sports, FantasyCalc and USA Today give receivers 6.9 to 8.5 points more of the pie than projections do (CBS Sports 8.5, FantasyCalc 7.2, USA Today 6.9). FantasyPros lands within 0.4 of a point.

Now press the scoring buttons. Projections move a lot: running backs hold 63.6% of the value in Standard and 41.9% in Full PPR. The Analyst charts (the average of CBS Sports, USA Today and FantasyPros) move less: 51.1% to 41.1%. In Full PPR everyone agrees within a few points. In Standard, the charts still price the league like it rewards catches. DynastyProcess, a dynasty tool, gives receivers the most of anyone: 46.7% in Half PPR (it prices future seasons, not just this one). One wrinkle: a chart's share can move with scoring even when its numbers do not, because our model changes how many backs and receivers start in each format.

In the charts' defense. Running backs miss more games, and a chart may price that risk more heavily than projections do. Our model already docks them for it: it expects running backs to miss 15.3% of games and receivers 10.5%. So the gap is not just injuries, but a chart that wants a bigger safety margin than ours is not wrong by definition.

2. The shape of the curve: how fast does value fall?

Line up every player from best to worst and divide by the #1 player. A steep curve says stars are rare and expensive. A flat one says depth is nearly as good. By the 24th player, CBS Sports has fallen to 38% of its #1, our model to 40%, FantasyCalc to 46%, FantasyPros to 51%, DynastyProcess to 55% and USA Today only to 65%. Toggle the scoring and watch which lines bend. Two of them never do.

3. Does the chart know your scoring?

Switch from Standard to Full PPR and the order of players should change: a running back who catches 60 passes should pass one who does not. We took the top 36 running backs, receivers and tight ends on each list and counted how many change places. The projections move most (ESPN 83%), but be clear about why: for ESPN and CBS rest-of-season the formats are our scoring of their projected catches and yards, while Razzball publishes its own column per format. USA Today moves 59% and CBS Sports 32%, both from separate published columns per format. FantasyCalc changes every number but keeps the order within each position, because each position gets one multiplier. FantasyPros changes nothing, and neither does DynastyProcess, which publishes one value set and says it assumes 12-team PPR.

How we checked that this is them, not us. FantasyCalc: our pipeline makes one request per format to its public values endpoint (ppr=0 for Standard, ppr=1 for Full PPR, 12 teams, one QB) and stores the published number untouched; the multiplier is in those raw responses, not in any adjustment of ours. We repeated the two requests live on 2026-11-07 and got the same pattern. In 8 captures from 2026-09-29 to 2026-11-07, only 1, the oldest (2026-09-29), reordered players (10 of the top 36 running backs), so this looks like a change on FantasyCalc's side around the start of October. FantasyPros: the week 9 article's four tables carry one Value column (plus 2QB and tight end premium columns) and no scoring selector (read 2026-11-07), and its numbers were identical across formats in every weekly snapshot from week 5 to week 9.

A fair note for CBS Sports and USA Today: their quarterback numbers are the same in every format because neither publishes a quarterback column per scoring format. We use CBS Sports' one-QB 4-point column and USA Today's one-QB column; neither chart's 6-point passing touchdown column is used here.

4. Superflex: the quarterback discount

All four redraft charts publish superflex or 2QB numbers for 32 to 38 quarterbacks, enough for a 12-team league. Credit where due. But move to superflex and those four lift quarterbacks to between 22.1% and 34.6% of all value. Projections through our model say 45%. Two things are true here. A superflex league puts about 24 quarterbacks in lineups each week, out of 32 NFL starters, and projected points make that scarcity look extreme. And we have not tested our superflex weighting against real superflex trades, so this is the test where our yardstick is least sure of itself.

5. Depth: does the chart reach the end of your bench?

Every position has a waiver line: the value of the best player you could pick up for free once every team has filled its starters and bench. League size and roster slots set it. With our default lineup, a 12-team league rosters about 20 quarterbacks, 65 running backs, 62 receivers and 21 tight ends, so the line sits at the next player at each position (10 teams: 18, 59, 48, 15; 14 teams: 24, 74, 73, 25). Value above that line is what our model calls value above waivers.

A chart's list should end near that line. If it stops short, real roster value is left unpriced. If it reaches past, it puts a price on players the waiver wire could replace for free. Here is where each list ends in a 12-team league.

  • CBS Sports stops short at every position: 13 QBs, 43 RBs, 43 WRs and 11 TEs. Its last running back, Devin Singletary, is still worth 8.8% of its top back, and 22 rostered backs get no number at all. CBS Sports covers them with a rule (any unlisted player is worth no more than four points), so in a bench-for-bench trade it cannot tell your 50th back from your 60th.
  • USA Today reaches past the line at every position. 5.3% of all its value goes to players beyond it, the most of any chart, and its waiver-level tight end is still worth 11.1% of its top tight end. Bench depth costs more on USA Today than a free pickup would.
  • FantasyPros is mixed: past the line at QB and WR, short at RB (52 of 65), near it at TE. Its receiver at the line is still worth 14.5% of its top receiver, so it pays real value for receivers you could find on waivers.
  • FantasyCalc ends closest to the line: near it at RB, and past it at QB, WR and TE with players worth almost nothing on its scale; only 0.6% of its value sits past the line.
  • DynastyProcess lists nearly everyone (it is a dynasty list), but most of those players are worth almost nothing on its scale; 3.1% of its value sits past the 12-team line.
  • Our model prices value above waivers by construction, so 0% of its value is past the line. Its fault runs the other way: it gives a value above zero to only 55 of the 65 running backs a league rosters, so it treats the last few bench backs (often handcuffs) as replaceable. And for the short charts we estimate the missing players ourselves (67 for CBS Sports this week), which prices value the chart itself did not.

6. The 2-for-1 test

Here is where curve shape stops being academic. Add up two chart numbers and compare them with one, and you have priced a 2-for-1. But the team that takes two players must cut one, and that player was worth something too. Subtract each player's waiver-level value and the math changes. On FantasyPros, 68% of mid-tier pairs beat a top-12 star on raw numbers and 45% after the subtraction. On USA Today, 61% and 42%. CBS Sports and FantasyCalc are steep enough that the subtraction barely matters. CBS Sports also states a rule for lopsided deals: take 10% off when one side gets at least two more players. DynastyProcess sits in between: 27% raw, 17% after.

Try one yourself. The default is a real week 9 comparison on raw chart numbers; tick the box to subtract the roster spot.

7. Where the charts disagree

The good news: the charts mostly agree on order. Across the 109 players all four list, rank correlations run from 0.831 to 0.925. The fights are about price, and about a handful of players. Pick a chart to see where it parts from the other three. Points above the dashed line are players this chart ranks higher than the other three do; points below, lower.

8. Freshness

Every source published new numbers every week from week 5 to week 9, and every one of them moved its top players. The analyst charts keep about a quarter to a half of their numbers exactly the same from one week to the next. FantasyCalc changed every one of those numbers every week. Nobody here is stale.

9. Age: what a dynasty value pays for

Our snapshot has ages for one source only: DynastyProcess publishes them in its open data file. So we can ask a fair question of it. Among the 149 of our top 150 players that DynastyProcess lists with an age, the 44 under 25 rank 3.8 places higher at their position on DynastyProcess than on our redraft value, on average. Players 30 and over come out at -3.6 places. That is the pattern you would expect from values that price future seasons, and it makes DynastyProcess a poor fit for a redraft trade: on the 109 players all four redraft charts list, its rank correlation with them runs from 0.524 (FantasyPros) to 0.727 (FantasyCalc), against 0.831 to 0.925 among the redraft charts themselves.

Source by source

What each source does well, where it falls short, and what it says about its own method. Method facts come only from pages the source published, read on 2026-11-07. Where we could not find or load a method page, we say so and make no claim.

CBS Sports Analyst chart

  • Does well: separate Standard, Half and Full PPR columns that really reorder players; a 2QB column; the steepest curve here, so 2-for-1 sums hold up; written rules for lopsided deals and unlisted players.
  • Falls short: lists 110 players, which stops well short of a 12-team bench; gives receivers the biggest tilt over projections (8.5 points in Half PPR); lowest quarterback share in superflex (22.1%).
  • Method, in its own words: Dave Richard's chart is based on expected future performance, upcoming schedule and public sentiment, and doubles as rest-of-season rankings. League size: not documented. [1]

USA Today Analyst chart

  • Does well: the deepest list (271 players), so it prices bench players itself; separate scoring columns that reorder 59% of the top 36; a superflex column for 38 quarterbacks.
  • Falls short: the flattest curve (the 24th player is worth 65% of the #1), so sums of two players flatter the side getting more; its top tight end at 80% of its #1 is far above every other chart.
  • Method: not documented in any page we could load. Its site did not allow our reader on 2026-11-07, so we describe only its published table as our pipeline saved it. [2]

FantasyPros Analyst chart

  • Does well: the position mix closest to projections in Half PPR (0.8 points apart); 2QB values for quarterbacks and a tight end premium column; prints each player's bye week.
  • Falls short: one Value column for every scoring format, so it cannot tell Standard from Full PPR; a flat curve that lets 68% of mid-tier pairs beat a star.
  • Method: the article says its analysts combined their rankings to make the values, and that "Values will be different league-to-league." Scoring format and league size: not stated. [3]

FantasyCalc Live market

  • Does well: takes league size as a setting; a 2QB setting with values for every player; a steep curve, so 2-for-1 sums barely need the roster-spot correction; new numbers for its top players every week.
  • Falls short: since early October its scoring switch rescales each position instead of reordering within it (section 3 shows how we checked): 0 of the top 99 move; leans toward receivers (7.2 points in Half PPR); lists 60 running backs where a 12-team league rosters about 65.
  • Method: its method page did not load for us on 2026-11-07, so we make no claim about how its values are built. Its public values request takes dynasty or redraft, number of quarterbacks, number of teams and PPR. [4]

DynastyProcess Dynasty, open data

  • Does well: the only source here that publishes both its formula and its data under an open license (GPL-3.0), so every number can be checked; 2QB values for 90 quarterbacks; the longest list of the graded sources (604 players we could match, against 110 to 271 for the charts); new values every week.
  • Falls short: one value set for every scoring format; it prices future seasons, so it is a poor fit for a redraft trade (it gives receivers 46.7% of the value in Half PPR, and its rank correlation with the redraft charts runs from 0.524 to 0.727 on the players all four list).
  • Method, in its own words: Value = 10,500 × e−0.0235 × rank, where rank is the FantasyPros dynasty expert consensus; 12-team PPR; it states no age adjustment of its own; method page last updated 2020-06-25. Its formula puts the 24th player at 58% of the #1. [8]

ESPN projections Projection

  • Does well: projects catches, yards and touchdowns, so it can be scored for any format (we do the scoring: 83% of the top 36 change places between Standard and Full PPR); prices hundreds of players.
  • Falls short: it is a set of projected points, not trade values, so every value we show from it is our model's translation. Our own checks flagged it red this week, so it is left out of DDF Value and the displayed values.
  • Method, in its own words: Mike Clay builds the projections team by team, not automatically, from shares of dropbacks, carries and targets, mixing statistics and judgment. [5]

Razzball projections Projection

  • Does well: publishes its own Standard, Half and Full PPR points-per-game columns, updated daily; lists its inputs openly.
  • Falls short: also projected points, translated by our model; our checks flagged it red this week, so it is left out of DDF Value and the displayed values.
  • Method, in its own words: a model backtested against past seasons, using roles and snaps, injury status, Vegas lines and more; it calls rookie projections without usage data a rough guess. [6]

The big names we could not test

We grade a source only when we can use its numbers legitimately. KeepTradeCut's terms prohibit automated data collection and redistributing its content [11]; Footballguys' terms prohibit scraping and copying without written permission [12]; Draft Sharks publishes no data license or reuse terms we could find, and its league-specific values come from its Draft War Room [10]. So these stay method-only, with no grades. We also looked at Dynasty Nerds but found no published method or data terms we could read, so it is left out.

KeepTradeCut Crowd votes

  • Users answer a daily keep, trade or cut question; the answers are combined with an ELO algorithm. A second mode builds values from real dynasty trades with a proprietary algorithm, and a third blends the two. [7]
  • Crowd values update every 10 minutes. Its own stated limits: trade data lags breaking news, and crowd values can overreact.
  • League settings the values assume: not documented on the page we loaded.

Footballguys Analyst chart

  • Dan Hindery's dynasty chart starts from points per game above replacement, discounted over a career, and is checked against about 90,000 Sleeper dynasty trades. [9]
  • Its base setting is superflex, Full PPR; the author says it is maintained by hand and that he adjusts it at times.

Draft Sharks Model

  • Its dynasty values come from models trained on player data since 1999, with aging curves by position, expressed as value above replacement. [10]
  • Baseline: 12 teams, with 1QB, superflex and tight end premium versions.

Our turn in the chair

It would be a strange audit that skipped the auditor. Here is where our own numbers are weakest this week.

  • We inherit the charts' opinions. DDF Value is the average of the four charts after our adjustments. When all four lean the same way on a player, so do we.
  • Our checks flagged 4 of 7 sources. USA Today and CBS rest-of-season got amber flags (still used); ESPN and Razzball got red ones (left out of DDF Value and the displayed values, though they still count in our league allocation and weights).
  • Our yardstick includes two flagged sources. Two of the three projection sets behind "projections" in this article, ESPN and Razzball, failed our own scrape checks this week.
  • We save every chart at 12 teams. For 8, 10 or 14 teams our model derives estimates from the 12-team chart, even for FantasyCalc, which offers other team counts we do not save.
  • We fill in missing players. This week we estimated 67 players for CBS Sports, 25 for FantasyPros and 14 for FantasyCalc. CBS Sports says an unlisted player is worth up to four points; our estimate puts a waiver-level CBS Sports running back at 0.34.
  • We could test age for one source only. Only DynastyProcess publishes ages; we have none for the other charts, no rookie test, and our injury list is newer than the charts, so an injury test would be unfair to them.
  • We match DynastyProcess by name, with the same name rule the rest of our pipeline uses. 604 players matched; 9 did not (six are not in our player data, three are listed at another position), and none is worth even 0.1% of its #1.
  • We have not tested our superflex weighting against real superflex trades. The quarterback discount in section 4 may be partly ours.

What this means for your trade

  1. In a 2-for-1, subtract the player you will cut. On FantasyPros and USA Today, adding two numbers makes depth look rich. CBS Sports and FantasyCalc are steep enough that it matters less.
  2. Check the scoring before you quote a chart. FantasyPros has one number for every format, and FantasyCalc's order within each position is the same in Standard and Full PPR. CBS Sports and USA Today publish a column per format; use the right one.
  3. In superflex, the charts price quarterbacks below what projected points imply through our model. If you are buying a quarterback, a chart helps your pitch; if you are selling one, it does not.
  4. Expect agreement on order, not on price. The charts rank players much the same; they disagree about exchange rates, especially for tight ends and for the second half of your roster.

Caveats

  • One snapshot: week 9 of 2026. Analyst charts published 2026-11-03 to 2026-11-04; FantasyCalc pulled 2026-11-07 (the freshness table uses its 2026-11-03 weekly snapshot). A different week could grade differently.
  • Tests use a 12-team league with our default lineup (QB, 2 RB, 2 WR, TE, FLEX, 7 bench) and Half PPR unless noted.
  • Position balance and the superflex comparison use our model as the yardstick. It is our point of view, and it has faults (above).
  • The grade thresholds are ours. They are written down so you can disagree with them precisely.
  • Method statements come from each source's own pages, read 2026-11-07. We do not guess at anything a source does not document.
